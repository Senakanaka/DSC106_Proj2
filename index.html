Assignment 4 Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Seiichi Nakahira — senakahira@ucsd.edu

Proposition: Global warming is directly responsible for the significant rise in sea levels since 1992.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Included Global Aggregates Only (Country = "World")
    Score: +2
    I chose to focus solely on global averages instead of individual countries or regions to emphasize the planet-wide impact of climate change. Including all regional data might have introduced noise or conflicting local patterns. The goal here was clarity: by highlighting overall trends rather than exceptions, the narrative becomes more persuasive and grounded in a macro-level reality. I considered breaking it down by ocean basins or hemispheres, but that would have distracted from the global climate signal.
  • Overlayed Dual Y-Axis Plot for Direct Visual Comparison
    Score: +1.5
    I used two y-axes (left: sea level; right: temperature) so that both variables could be shown simultaneously without needing normalization or rescaling. This choice helps viewers immediately see how sea level rise and global temperature have moved in tandem over the past few decades. It’s a common and accepted method, but I recognize it can be slightly misleading if scales aren’t chosen carefully. In this case, the ranges were selected to show trends clearly without exaggerating.
  • Displayed Full Time Span (1992–2020) Without Truncation
    Score: +2
    I made a deliberate choice to display the full range of available years rather than cherry-picking a subset. This was important because the most compelling evidence for climate change is in the recent sharp increases. Cutting the data early (like in 2005) would obscure this pattern. Using the complete data helps maintain analytical integrity while making the upward trend visually undeniable.
  • Did Not Apply Smoothing or Trend Lines
    Score: +1.5
    Although the graph might look cleaner with a regression line or rolling average, I wanted to preserve the volatility and raw character of the real-world data. These fluctuations remind viewers that natural variability exists, but they still reveal an unmistakable upward trend. I debated adding a regression fit, but ultimately felt the dual-line format made the relationship strong enough on its own.
  • Used Color Encoding for Intuitive Contrast
    Score: +1.5
    I assigned blue for sea level and red for temperature to intuitively match common associations (blue = water; red = heat). This helps viewers distinguish the two metrics quickly and accurately. It’s a subtle design decision, but it contributes to the ease and speed of understanding the data relationships.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Use same y-axis scale for both variables
    Score: -1.5
    By plotting sea level rise (in millimeters) and temperature (in degrees Celsius) on the same y-axis scale, the graph visually flattens the temperature trend. While sea level values range widely, temperature values only fluctuate slightly between ~1.0 and ~1.6 °C, making the red temperature line appear nearly flat. This undermines the perceived relationship between the two variables and downplays the seriousness of global warming. What worked well is that the steep sea level line dominates the viewer’s attention. However, this choice misrepresents the actual upward trend in temperature. I considered using a dual-axis plot to more accurately represent each variable, but opted for a unified scale to subtly distort the comparison in support of a skeptical narrative.
  • Title: “Dramatic Climate Shift: Sea Levels and Temperatures Surge”
    Score: -0.5
    The title uses emotionally charged language (“Dramatic” and “Surge”) to suggest a strong, urgent trend. However, the actual temperature line appears mostly flat due to scale compression. This mismatch between the visual and the wording may confuse or mislead viewers. It’s persuasive framing that steers interpretation before a viewer even reads the axes. A more neutral title might have been “Sea Level and Temperature Trends (1992–2020),” but I opted for this dramatic title to amplify a sense of crisis, even if the graph doesn’t fully support it.
  • Plot both variables with different units on same axis
    Score: -1.5
    Temperature is measured in degrees Celsius and sea level in millimeters—two completely different units and magnitudes. Plotting them on the same axis implicitly equates their scales and exaggerates sea level rise while minimizing temperature change. It makes the red line nearly flat while the blue line looks alarmingly steep. This distorts interpretation and weakens the perceived correlation between the two. A dual-axis approach would have provided more clarity, but I kept the unified axis to visually bias the viewer’s focus.
  • Do not label y-axis units explicitly
    Score: -1
    The y-axis label “Measurement” fails to indicate the actual units (°C for temperature and mm for sea level), obscuring the fact that these are on vastly different scales. Without unit labels, viewers may assume both metrics operate on the same scale, which is misleading. I considered labeling the y-axes properly, but omitting units helped make the flattening of temperature appear more natural and less questionable.

Final Reflection

After creating both the persuasive and deceptive visualizations, I realized how easily the same dataset can tell two completely different stories depending on design choices. In my earnest graph, I used a dual-axis plot to clearly show how global temperature rise and sea level increase have moved in parallel over the years. This approach made it obvious that the Earth's warming is not just a number—it’s causing real, irreversible changes, such as the melting of polar ice caps. Countries like Tuvalu are literally sinking, and polar animals like seals and polar bears are losing their habitats and sources of survival.

In contrast, my deceptive visualization plotted both variables on the same y-axis, despite their different units and scales. This made the temperature trend look flat and unimportant, even though the data shows it rising steadily. The steep sea level curve visually dominated the graph, distracting from the relationship between the two variables. What surprised me most was how simple it was to manipulate viewer perception—not by changing the data, but by changing how it was presented.

Through this project, I came to define ethical analysis and visualization as the practice of representing truth transparently, without distorting the data’s message to mislead an audience. The line between persuasion and deception lies in the designer’s intent and the transparency of their choices. As the famous quote often attributed to Mark Twain says, “Numbers don’t lie, but liars use numbers.” I believe this project gave me a hands-on experience with that idea—demonstrating how even honest data can be twisted to tell a dishonest story.
